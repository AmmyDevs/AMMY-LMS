## Point Estimation

**Point estimation** uses a single sample statistic to estimate an unknown population parameter. It provides a "best guess" value.

| Population Parameter | Point Estimator |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| Population mean $\mu$ | Sample mean $\bar{x}$ |
| Population proportion $p$ | Sample proportion $\hat{p}$ |
| Population variance $\sigma^2$ | Sample variance $s^2$ |

<Flashcard front="What is point estimation?" back="Using a **single sample statistic** (like $\\bar{x}$) to estimate an unknown **population parameter** (like $\\mu$)." />

---

## Interval Estimation

**Interval estimation** provides a range of values (a **confidence interval**) that is likely to contain the true population parameter. This accounts for the uncertainty inherent in using a sample.

### Margin of Error

The **margin of error** is the distance from the point estimate to the endpoints of the confidence interval.

<Flashcard front="What is the margin of error?" back="The **distance** from the point estimate to the endpoints of the confidence interval — it quantifies the uncertainty of the estimate." />

---

## Confidence Intervals for Population Mean

### Case 1: Population Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) Known

When $\sigma$ is known, we use the **Standard Normal (Z) distribution**.

$$\text{CI} = \bar{x} \pm Z_c \times \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

### Common Critical Values

| Confidence Level | $Z_c$ Value |
|-----------------|-------------|
| 90% | 1.645 |
| 95% | 1.960 |
| 99% | 2.578 |

<Flashcard front="What is the formula for a confidence interval when σ is known?" back="$\\bar{x} \\pm Z_c \\times \\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}}$ where $Z_c$ is the critical value from the standard normal distribution." />

### Case 2: Population Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) Unknown

When $\sigma$ is unknown, we use the **Student's t-distribution** with the sample standard deviation $s$.

$$\text{CI} = \bar{x} \pm t_c \times \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

<Flashcard front="When do you use the t-distribution instead of Z?" back="When the **population standard deviation $\\sigma$ is unknown** and the sample size is small ($n < 30$)." />

---

## Student's t-Distribution

The t-distribution was introduced by William Sealy Gosset to handle the extra uncertainty when using $s$ instead of $\sigma$.

### Properties

- **Continuous** and **bell-shaped** (like Z)
- **Symmetrical** about zero (mean = 0)
- **Flatter and more spread out** than Z
- Shape depends on **degrees of freedom** ($df = n - 1$)
- **Approaches Z** as $n$ increases

| Characteristic | Z Distribution | t Distribution |
|---------------|----------------|----------------|
| Symmetric | Yes | Yes |
| Bell-shaped | Yes | Yes |
| Mean | 0 | 0 |
| Spread | Narrower | Wider |
| Depends on $n$ | No | Yes (via $df$) |

<Flashcard front="How does the t-distribution differ from the Z distribution?" back="The t-distribution is **flatter and more spread out** than Z, with heavier tails. Its shape depends on degrees of freedom ($df = n - 1$), and it **approaches Z** as $n$ increases." />

---

## Decision Matrix: Z vs. t

| Sample Size | $\sigma$ Known | $\sigma$ Unknown |
|-------------|----------------|-------------------|
| $n \geq 30$ | Use $Z$ | Use $Z$ (approximate) |
| $n < 30$ | Use $Z$ | Use $t$ |

<Flashcard front="When should you use Z vs. t?" back="Use **Z** when $\\sigma$ is known or $n \\geq 30$. Use **t** when $\\sigma$ is unknown AND $n < 30$." />

---

## Key Principles

1. **Increasing the confidence level** always increases the margin of error
2. **A 100% confidence interval** is impossible without a full census
3. **Larger sample sizes** produce narrower confidence intervals
4. The confidence interval gives a **range of plausible values** for the parameter

<Flashcard front="Why can't you have a 100% confidence interval?" back="Because to be 100% confident, you would need to include **all possible values** — which is equivalent to a full census and provides no useful information." />

<Flashcard front="What happens to the confidence interval when you increase the confidence level?" back="The interval becomes **wider** — a higher confidence level requires capturing more of the distribution to ensure the parameter is included." />
