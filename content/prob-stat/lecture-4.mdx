## Introduction to Probability

Probability is a **measure of uncertainty** that ranges from 0 (impossible event) to 1 (certain event). It provides a mathematical framework for reasoning about events whose outcomes are not known with certainty.

A **random experiment** is any process whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty. The set of all possible outcomes is called the **sample space** $\Omega$. Any subset of the sample space is called an **event**.

<Flashcard front="What is probability?" back="Probability is a **measure of uncertainty** that ranges from 0 (impossible event) to 1 (certain event)." />

<Flashcard front="What is a sample space?" back="The sample space $\Omega$ is the set of **all possible outcomes** of a random experiment." />

## Key Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Random Experiment** | A process whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty |
| **Sample Space ($\Omega$)** | The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment |
| **Event** | A subset of the sample space |
| **Sure Event** | An event that includes all outcomes in the sample space |
| **Impossible Event** | An event with no outcomes (empty set $\emptyset$) |
| **Equally Likely Events** | Events with the same probability of occurring |
| **Mutually Exclusive Events** | Events that cannot occur simultaneously |
| **Exhaustive Events** | A collection of events whose union equals the sample space |
| **Dependent Events** | Events where the occurrence of one affects the probability of another |

<Flashcard front="What are mutually exclusive events?" back="Events that **cannot occur simultaneously** — if one occurs, the other cannot." />

<Flashcard front="What is the difference between exhaustive and mutually exclusive events?" back="**Exhaustive** events cover all possible outcomes (their union = $\Omega$). **Mutually exclusive** events cannot occur together (their intersection = $\emptyset$)." />

## Permutations and Combinations

### Permutations

Permutations count the number of **arrangements** where order matters.

$$P(n, r) = \frac{n!}{(n - r)!}$$

### Combinations

Combinations count the number of **selections** where order does not matter.

$$C(n, r) = \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n - r)!}$$

<Flashcard front="When do you use permutations vs. combinations?" back="Use **permutations** when the **order matters** (arrangements). Use **combinations** when the **order does not matter** (selections/groupings)." />

## Schools of Thought on Probability

There are four main approaches to defining probability:

### 1. Classical (A Priori)

Probability is the ratio of favorable cases to total equally likely cases.

$$P(A) = \frac{\text{Number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of equally likely outcomes}}$$

### 2. Relative Frequency (Empirical)

Probability is based on the frequency of occurrence in a large number of trials.

$$P(A) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f_A}{n}$$

where $f_A$ is the frequency of event $A$ in $n$ trials.

### 3. Subjective (Personalistic)

Probability is based on personal belief, confidence, or degree of belief.

### 4. Axiomatic (Modern)

Probability is defined through mathematical axioms:
- $P(A) \geq 0$ for any event $A$
- $P(\Omega) = 1$
- For mutually exclusive events: $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$

<Flashcard front="What are the four schools of thought on probability?" back="**Classical** (ratio of outcomes), **Relative Frequency** (long-run observations), **Subjective** (personal belief), and **Axiomatic** (mathematical axioms)." />

## Addition Theorem

The addition theorem gives the probability of the union of two events.

### Mutually Exclusive Events

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

### Not Mutually Exclusive

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

<Flashcard front="What is the addition theorem for mutually exclusive events?" back="$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$ — since they cannot occur together, you simply add their probabilities." />

<Flashcard front="Why do we subtract P(A ∩ B) in the general addition theorem?" back="Because $P(A) + P(B)$ **double-counts** the overlap. Subtracting $P(A \cap B)$ corrects for this." />

---

## Multiplication Theorem

The multiplication theorem gives the probability of the intersection of two events.

### Independent Events

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$$

### Dependent Events

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A)$$

where $P(B|A)$ is the conditional probability of $B$ given $A$ has occurred.

<Flashcard front="What is the difference between the multiplication rules for independent and dependent events?" back="For **independent** events: $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$. For **dependent** events: $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A)$." />

---

## Conditional Probability

Conditional probability is the probability of an event given that another event has already occurred.

$$P(B|A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}$$

This is fundamental to understanding Bayes' Theorem.

<Flashcard front="What is conditional probability?" back="The probability of event $B$ **given that event $A$ has occurred**, calculated as $P(B|A) = \\frac{P(A \\cap B)}{P(A)}$." />

---

## Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' Theorem allows us to **revise prior probabilities** into posterior probabilities based on new evidence.

$$P(A_i|B) = \frac{P(B|A_i) \cdot P(A_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(B|A_j) \cdot P(A_j)}$$

Where:
- $P(A_i)$ — **Prior probability** (before revision)
- $P(A_i|B)$ — **Posterior probability** (after revision)
- $P(B|A_i)$ — **Likelihood** of observing $B$ given $A_i$

<Flashcard front="What does Bayes' Theorem allow you to do?" back="It allows you to **revise prior probabilities** into **posterior probabilities** based on new evidence or information." />

<Flashcard front="What is the difference between a prior and posterior probability?" back="A **prior** probability is the probability **before** considering new evidence. A **posterior** probability is the probability **after** incorporating new evidence via Bayes' Theorem." />
