## The Empirical Rule

The Empirical Rule describes how data is distributed around the mean in a normal distribution:

| Range | Percentage |
|-------|------------|
| $\mu \pm 1\sigma$ | ≈ 68% |
| $\mu \pm 2\sigma$ | ≈ 95% |
| $\mu \pm 3\sigma$ | ≈ 99% |

This rule helps identify whether a data point is typical or unusual relative to the rest of the data.

<Flashcard front="What does the Empirical Rule state?" back="Approximately **68%** of data falls within 1 standard deviation, **95%** within 2, and **99%** within 3 standard deviations of the mean." />

---

## Z-Scores

A **Z-score** measures how many standard deviations a data point is from the mean:

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$

### Interpretation

| Z-Score Range | Interpretation |
|---------------|----------------|
| $-1 < z < 1$ | Typical / common value |
| $\|z\| > 2$ | Unusual value |
| $\|z\| > 3$ | Extremely unusual value |

<Flashcard front="What does a Z-score of -2 mean?" back="The data point is **2 standard deviations below** the mean — an unusual value." />

## The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

The CLT is one of the most important theorems in statistics. It states that for a sufficiently large sample size $n$, the **sample mean $\bar{X}$ is approximately normally distributed**, regardless of the shape of the population distribution.

### Key Properties

1. The **mean** of the sample mean equals the population mean:
$$\mu_{\bar{x}} = \mu$$

2. The **standard deviation** of the sample mean (standard error) decreases as $n$ increases:
$$\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

3. The distribution of $\bar{X}$ approaches Normal as $n$ increases.

### CLT Formula

$$Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

### Demonstration: Dice Experiment

Rolling a single die gives a **uniform distribution** (each value 1-6 equally likely). But as we increase the sample size:

| Sample Size ($n$) | Distribution of $\bar{X}$ |
|-------------------|--------------------------|
| $n = 1$ | Uniform (flat) |
| $n = 2$ | Slightly bell-shaped |
| $n = 10$ | Approximately normal |
| $n = 30$ | Very close to normal |

<Flashcard front="What does the Central Limit Theorem state?" back="For a sufficiently large $n$, the **sample mean $\\bar{X}$ is approximately normally distributed**, regardless of the population distribution." />

<Flashcard front="What is the formula for the standard error of the mean?" back="$\\sigma_{\\bar{x}} = \\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}}$ — the standard deviation of the sample mean decreases as sample size increases." />

<Flashcard front="Why does the CLT matter?" back="It allows us to use the **Normal distribution** to make inferences about population means, even when the population itself is not normally distributed." />

---

## Sample Mean as a Random Variable

The sample mean $\bar{X}$ is itself a random variable because it varies from sample to sample.

### Key Observations from the Dice Experiment

1. The **mean stays constant** regardless of sample size
2. The **standard deviation decreases** as $n$ increases ($\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$)
3. The **distribution becomes more normal** as $n$ increases

<Flashcard front="What happens to the standard error as sample size increases?" back="The standard error **decreases** — specifically, it equals $\\frac{\\sigma}{\\sqrt{n}}$, so larger samples give more precise estimates." />

---

## Practical Application

The CLT enables **inferential statistics** by allowing us to:
- Estimate population parameters from sample statistics
- Construct confidence intervals for population means
- Perform hypothesis tests about population means

### Example

If the mean height of American men is $\mu = 69$ inches with $\sigma = 2.8$ inches, and we take a sample of $n = 50$:

$$\sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{2.8}{\sqrt{50}} \approx 0.396$$

The probability that the sample mean exceeds 70 inches can be calculated using:

$$Z = \frac{70 - 69}{0.396} \approx 2.53$$

This gives $P(\bar{X} > 70) = P(Z > 2.53) \approx 0.0057$ — very unlikely.
